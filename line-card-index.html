What “best” and “worst” mean here

A 30-year-old agency can be an outstanding sales organization and still publish a card that fights itself. A smaller firm can look modest on the website and still have a card an engineer can understand in one sitting. We scored public line-card coherence only: does the published mix share customers and specification paths, or does it collect unrelated catalogs?

Industries in this index: industrial OEM and automation, aerospace and defense electronics, medical electronics and devices, and electronics / interconnect / power / RF.

The “best” list names real firms whose public cards, as of August 2026, tell one technical story (focused categories, named territory, complementary rather than competing layers). Inclusion is not an endorsement of revenue, ethics, or coverage quality. We did not audit their CRM or ride with their people.

The “worst” list does not name living agencies. Ranking a neighbor’s firm as a failure on the open internet is a cheap way to get clicks and a poor way to keep friends in this industry. Instead we scored ten common published-card patterns we see on real websites. If your card looks like one of them, that is the point of the list. Run the free check on your own URLs.

Worst of the common patterns

10 published-card types that score poorly—on purpose

Composite examples. Names in quotes are labels, not companies. If this sounds like the PDF in your bag, that is useful information, not an insult.

#PatternWhat the published card usually signalsScore
1 “We cover the United States” with two outside people Territory is a slogan. Factories cannot tell who actually shows up in Ohio versus Oregon. 22
2 Two competing power-supply (or connector, or sensor) principals Same slot, two factories. Every design-in becomes a choice of whom to starve. 24
3 Mil-aero and lawn-and-garden on one PDF No shared specification path. The card is a filing cabinet, not a story. 26
4 Forty-plus principals, no category grouping An engineer cannot tell what you actually know. Neither can a new factory. 28
5 Consumer gadgets next to implantable or flight hardware Quality systems, call patterns, and risk tolerance do not match. Principals in the hard industries notice. 30
6 Distribution house-brand sitting in the same layer as an exclusive OEM line Published conflict. Even if you “know how to manage it,” the website has already told on you. 32
7 “All markets: aerospace, medical, semiconductor, appliance, gifts, construction…” Keyword stuffing for search, not a call plan. Dilutes every factory equally. 34
8 No named territory, no named outside people, only a web form A principal cannot diligence coverage. Score collapses before products are even discussed. 36
9 Card last updated in 2014, still listing lines that left Public record is stale. That is a factory-relations problem hiding in plain sight. 38
10 Three nearly identical “me-too” accessory lines around one strong principal Looks complementary; behaves like clutter. Mindshare at the counter goes to whoever shouts. 39

The scale

70–84 Strong

Clear focus, a few gaps or adjacency questions worth asking in writing.

55–69 Moderate

Usable, but the mix needs pruning or a tighter territory story.

40–54 Limited

Published card is working against the next appointment.

0–39 Watch

Conflict, sprawl, or coverage claims the site cannot support.

How a firm got on the “best” list

We looked at publicly posted line cards and “markets we serve” pages. Higher scores went to cards that (1) stay inside a related set of technologies, (2) name a territory a factory can verify, (3) do not list obvious competitors in the same slot, and (4) match industrial, aerospace, medical, or electronics work rather than every SIC code. We did not count LinkedIn follower counts, booth size, or years in business as a substitute for a coherent card.
